Typeferry privacy policy

Last updated: 2026-10-20

Developer: Daniel Alexis Cruz

Typeferry turns your phone into a keyboard, a mouse and a set of shortcut keys for your own computer. This page says what the app does with your information. The short version: it collects nothing and sends nothing to anyone but your own PC.

What the app collects

Nothing. The app has no account, no sign-in, no advertising, no analytics, no crash reporting and no tracking of any kind. It does not contact any server run by its developer. There is no data for the developer to see, sell or share.

What stays on your phone

  • Your keyboard profiles (the keys you design, their colours and layers) and your settings are stored in the app's private storage on your phone. Android removes them when you uninstall the app.
  • The pairing code that links your phone to your PC is stored there too, in the app's private settings (under the name pc_token). It is never sent over the network (the two devices only prove to each other that they know it) and never leaves the two devices that share it. Android's cloud backup is switched off for this app, so the code is not copied to a Google backup.
  • The address of the last PC connected over Bluetooth, so the app can reconnect to it. It stays on the phone.

What the app sends, and to where

The app sends the keys you press, the mouse movements you make on its trackpad and the text you ask it to type to the PC you paired it with, and only to that PC. It does this over Bluetooth, over your own Wi-Fi network, or over a USB cable. Over Wi-Fi and USB the link is authenticated with the pairing code and every message is encrypted and tamper-checked. Nothing goes through the internet and nothing goes to the developer or to any third party.

Besides the keys themselves, the phone tells your PC its own network addresses (so a lost connection can be found again quickly), sends a regular heartbeat, and tells it whether app switching is on. The PC in turn tells the phone the state of its Caps, Num and Scroll Lock keys, whether it is locked, and the name of the program in front (as a hash, only while app switching is on). A PC can ask to pair only during the three minutes after you open the pairing step in the setup guide, and it is accepted only if you type the six digit number the PC shows into the phone and then type the second number the phone shows into the PC. Pairing a new PC replaces the old pairing.

If you turn on app switching, your PC tells the phone a short fingerprint of the name of the program in front, never its window title or contents, so the phone can pick a matching profile. The fingerprint is not stored.

Permissions the app asks for, and why

  • Nearby devices (Bluetooth connect and advertise): to appear to your PC as a Bluetooth keyboard and to connect to it. The app asks for it only when you choose Bluetooth (in the setup guide's Bluetooth route, or when you tap Connect, Pair or Bluetooth only in the menu). If you only use Wi-Fi or a USB cable it is never asked for.
  • Change network state: a permission Android grants at install, with no question to you. The connection service that carries the Wi-Fi and USB link needs one permission of this kind to start, so this one is declared and the Bluetooth permission is not needed for it. The app does not change any network setting.
  • Notifications: to show the one persistent notice, with Stop and Reconnect buttons, that tells you the keyboard connection service is running. The app asks once, after your first successful link, or together with the Nearby devices question in the Bluetooth step of the setup guide on Android 13 and later. If you refuse, the connection still works and Android lists the service in its own task manager.
  • Network access: to talk to your PC's receiver program over your own Wi-Fi or a USB cable. It is not used to reach the internet.
  • Keep awake (wake lock): lets the app hold a Wi-Fi lock while it listens for your PC, and keep the processor awake only while a PC is connected, so typing stays instant and the PC finds the phone again.
  • Run at startup (receive boot completed): a permission Android grants at install, with no question to you. It lets the app start its connection service again after the phone restarts, unless you tapped Stop.
  • Foreground service (type: connected device): keeps the link to your PC and the Bluetooth keyboard running while you use the app or another app and a PC is connected, so typing does not stop when you switch apps. It shows a notice with Stop and Reconnect buttons. It keeps running until you tap Stop, so your PC can find the phone again by itself, for example after the screen has been off for a night; a timer in the menu can stop it sooner. It starts again after the phone restarts or the app updates, unless you tapped Stop. The Stop button ends it at once.

The app reads the clipboard only when you tap Paste text, Import from clipboard, or the Paste button for a pairing code, and only to use that text. It puts text on the clipboard only when you tap Copy link, Share (copy as text) or Copy all my profiles, so you can paste it somewhere else. The Share link button opens Android's own share sheet: what you pick there leaves the app because you chose it. It never reads the clipboard in the background.

The app listens on one local port (8777) for your PC's receiver program. To find the phone, that program tries port 8777 on addresses of your own local network (including a USB or hotspot tether) and contacts nothing on the internet. A device can connect only by proving it knows the pairing code. On Android 11 and older the older Bluetooth and Bluetooth admin permissions serve the same keyboard link. Voice and swipe typing use your phone's own keyboard, which has its own privacy terms.

The receiver program on your PC (a separate download) looks for your phone only on the networks your PC is on, sends nothing to the internet, and keeps its files on your PC: a copy of the program itself (about 12 MB, in the Typeferry folder inside your local app data), the pairing code (a file named token.txt), the address of your phone, a short log of connections (addresses and events, never the keys you type) and a few small settings. After you open it once it starts with Windows from that copy (a per-user entry that you can remove from its tray icon menu with Remove from startup, or by running it with --uninstall; delete the Typeferry folder afterwards). While it runs it shows a tray icon and, when you click the icon, a status window; Quit in the tray menu ends it. The Typeferry folder belongs to your Windows account: other ordinary accounts on the PC cannot open it (an administrator of the PC can), and any program you run as yourself can read token.txt, the same way it could already press keys for you. To forget the pairing, delete token.txt: while the receiver runs it notices within about ten seconds and makes a new code, so the phone must be paired again and an old or lost phone stops working over Wi-Fi and USB; a phone that was also paired over Bluetooth stays paired in Windows until you remove Typeferry from your Bluetooth devices there (or quit the receiver and delete the whole folder). If the Android platform tools (adb) are installed on your PC, the receiver also asks adb every 15 seconds about a phone plugged in by USB cable: it lists the connected devices, reads the phone's model name and forwards port 8777 to the phone, and with --push-code it hands the pairing code to that phone over the same cable; it installs nothing and contacts nothing else.

Children

The app is not directed at children and collects no information from anyone, children included.
